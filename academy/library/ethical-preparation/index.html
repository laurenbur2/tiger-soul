What ethical preparation means

Preparation is everything that happens before a person sits down. It becomes ethical when it is genuinely capable of arriving at no.

The word doing the work

Most people offering this work will say they prepare participants. Very few mean the same thing by it. For some it is a welcome email and a reminder not to eat beforehand. For others it is three months of screening, medical review, written agreements, and a standing willingness to return someone's money and send them home.

The word ethical is there to mark the difference. Preparation asks how a person gets ready. Ethical preparation asks a harder set of questions: whether this should happen at all, on whose terms, who benefits from the answer, and what happens to the person if it goes wrong.

A practical test. If a preparation process has never resulted in someone being turned away, it is not a preparation process. It is an onboarding sequence.

Screening that can say no

Ethical preparation starts with a complete and unflattering history. Medical, psychiatric, medications, substances, family history, and what has been happening in the person's life over the last year. Not a checkbox form. A conversation, with follow-up questions, conducted by someone competent to understand the answers.

Two structural points matter more than the questions themselves.

  • The person conducting the screening should not be the person whose income depends on the answer. Where an organisation is too small for that separation, it has to be named out loud and compensated for deliberately.
  • Screening has to happen before payment is taken, or with a full refund guaranteed in writing. Otherwise the participant has a financial reason to shade their answers, and so does the organiser.

The subjects that get skipped most often are cardiovascular history, current psychiatric medication, a family history of psychosis or bipolar disorder, and how much the person is actually drinking. See screening and contraindications.

Consent that is actually informed

Consent is harder here than in almost any other setting, because nobody can fully describe in advance what the person is consenting to. That is not a reason to lower the standard. It is a reason to be much more specific about everything that can be described.

At minimum, in writing and in advance, the participant should know what is being offered and where it came from, what is known about it and what is not, the physical and psychological risks in plain language, who will be in the room and what each of them is responsible for, whether touch is part of the practice and how they can decline or withdraw it at any point, what happens if they want to stop, and what the refund policy is if they change their mind.

A consent form signed on arrival, after travel, after payment, in a room full of people who have already committed, is a legal artifact rather than a meaningful one. Send it weeks earlier and invite questions. See consent, power, and money.

The right reason, held loosely

A large part of preparing someone is helping them hear what they are actually asking for.

Intentions that tend to hold up are small and specific. To be able to grieve a particular person. To stop repeating one pattern. To look honestly at something already known. Intentions that tend not to hold up are large and outsourced. To be fixed. To be told what to do with my life. To become someone else.

Some of the people who arrive are asking for something a ceremony does not provide. A person in an unsafe living situation needs that addressed first. A person in acute crisis needs care, not an intense experience. A person who has been to six ceremonies in a year and is planning a seventh is describing a pattern, and the ethical response is to name it rather than book them in.

Aftercare arranged before, not after

The most common failure in this field is not a dramatic one. It is a person who has a significant experience, goes home, and has nobody to talk to about it. Two weeks later they are either trying to reconstruct the state or pretending nothing happened.

Ethical preparation answers, before the person arrives, who they will speak with in the first week and who in the first month. It confirms they have cleared the following few days. It asks whether they have a therapist, and encourages telling that person beforehand rather than afterward. Where the participant has nobody, that is a preparation problem to solve, not a detail to discover later. See what ethical integration means.

The weeks beforehand

The unglamorous part, and the part that changes the most. Reduced alcohol and cannabis, real sleep, regular movement, written reflection, and the conversation the person has been avoiding for a year. Practitioners consistently observe that a meaningful share of what participants later credit to a ceremony actually began here.

This is also where a person finds out whether they are willing to do anything at all. Someone unwilling to change a single habit for three weeks is telling you something useful about what they expect a few hours to do on their behalf.

The participant-facing version of this is in preparing yourself.

The willingness to say no

Everything above collapses without this one. A screening process is only as good as the organisation's willingness to act on it, and acting on it costs money every single time.

Not right now is a complete answer and often the correct one. Someone three weeks into a bereavement, someone whose medication needs a prescriber's involvement, someone who has just lost a job and is looking for a rescue, someone who cannot say in their own words why they are doing this. None of those is a permanent no. All of them are a not yet.

The organisations that take this seriously tend to be the ones that can point to specific occasions when they returned a deposit and sent someone home. Ask for those occasions. Watch whether the answer is a story or a slogan.
