Designing the container

Container is often used to mean candles and intention. Used properly it means a set of decisions made in advance about who is responsible for what, and what happens when something goes wrong.

Beyond atmosphere

Set and setting entered the language to describe how much a person's state of mind and their surroundings shape an experience. It is true and it has been flattened into decor.

The version that matters is procedural. Before anyone arrives, a responsible container has answers to: who is in the room and what each of them is accountable for, how a participant signals distress, who makes the call in an emergency, where the nearest hospital is and how long it takes to reach, what the consent agreement says about touch and about stopping, who is awake overnight, and what gets written down.

None of that is atmosphere. All of it decides whether the atmosphere holds.

Roles and ratios

Every person working in the room should have one clear job and know what is outside it. The person holding overall responsibility should not also be the person managing logistics, taking payments, or handling the music, because attention is finite and the moments that matter are brief.

Ratios should be conservative enough that any participant can be given individual attention for an extended period without the room being left unattended. A room where one person's difficulty consumes all available staff is a room where the rest have been quietly abandoned.

Someone should be designated, by name and in advance, to make the call if a situation becomes medical. Not by consensus in the moment.

The physical setting

Practical, not aesthetic. A surface that is safe to fall on. Clear routes to bathrooms. No sharp corners at head height. Temperature control, because people get very cold and very hot. Water. Lighting that can be adjusted without anyone crossing the room. A quieter space available for someone who needs to leave the group. Phone signal or a working landline. A charged phone with emergency numbers already in it.

Privacy that cuts both ways: participants should not be observable by strangers, and staff should not be alone and unobserved with a participant in a way that protects neither of them.

The stopping agreement

Agreed in advance, in writing, and rehearsed: what a participant does if they want the support to change or stop. A word, a gesture, a raised hand. Whatever it is, everyone working in the room knows it, and it is honoured immediately and without discussion.

The corollary is that no participant should ever be persuaded to continue with something they have signalled to stop. Reassurance in that moment is coercion with a soft voice.

Medical backup

Three things, decided before the day. Current first aid and resuscitation training among the staff present. A written emergency plan that names the hospital, the route, the travel time, and who drives. A completed medical summary for each participant, accessible immediately, that can be handed to a paramedic without anyone searching for it.

Remote and beautiful settings are common in this field and they extend transfer times considerably. That trade-off should be stated to participants during preparation rather than discovered in an emergency.

Group dynamics

A group can be steadying and it can be pressure. The same room that makes someone feel less alone can make it very hard to say no, to leave, or to admit that nothing is happening.

Things that help: participants told beforehand that they can decline anything, leave at any point, and that nobody will comment. Sharing circles where passing is genuinely ordinary. No public accounting of who did what. Attention paid to the quiet person as well as the loud one. And a clear norm that other people's material is not repeated outside the room.

Documentation

Unglamorous and protective for everyone. Screening records, signed consent, medication lists, emergency contacts, a note of anything unusual that happened, and what follow-up was arranged. Stored securely, retained for a defined period, and never used for marketing.

Organisations that keep no records cannot learn from what went wrong, cannot demonstrate what they did, and cannot hand a clinician the information they need. See consent, power, and money.
