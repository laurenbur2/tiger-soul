The first ninety days

Whatever happened in the room, the part that changes a life happens afterward, slowly, in an ordinary week.

  • Months two and three

The first week

Expect to be tired, emotionally thin, and easily overwhelmed by noise and other people. Expect your memory of the experience to be less clear than you assumed it would be, and to keep changing as you describe it.

What helps in this week is boring: sleep, food, water, daylight, walking, low stimulation, and very little screen time. What does not help is making large decisions. The urge to quit the job, end the relationship, sell the house, or announce a new direction is common and frequently regretted. Write the decision down instead and revisit it in a month. If it is right, it will still be right.

Talk to one person who knew you before. Not an audience.

The first month

The common shape is a lift for two or three weeks followed by a flattening, which people often read as failure. It usually is not. The lift is the residue of the experience. What comes after it is ordinary life, which is where the actual work has to happen.

This is the point at which one small commitment matters more than a large intention. One conversation that has been postponed. One habit reduced. One appointment made. Something checkable in seven days.

Re-read whatever you wrote beforehand. People are routinely surprised by how much of the insight they credit to the experience was already on that page.

Months two and three

Old patterns come back. This is expected and is not evidence that nothing happened. The difference a good experience makes is usually not that the pattern disappears, but that you can see it while it is happening rather than afterward.

The useful question in this period is not what did it mean. It is what is different. Who have I spoken to that I would not have. What am I no longer pretending about. What did I say I would do, and did I do it.

What reliably helps

  • Structure. Regular sleep, regular meals, regular movement. The least romantic part and the most load-bearing.
  • Writing. Short and frequent beats long and occasional. Memory of these experiences erodes faster than almost anyone expects.
  • One or two people. Someone who knew you before and will say something inconvenient if they see you drifting.
  • Therapy. Especially if something specific surfaced. It does not need to be a therapist who works with these practices, only one who will not dismiss the account.
  • Time outside. Unglamorous, consistently reported, costs nothing.

What quietly does not help

  • Telling the story repeatedly. Each retelling smooths it. After a while you are integrating a story rather than an experience.
  • Living online in it. Communities organised entirely around these experiences tend to reward intensity and discourage the return to ordinary life.
  • Scheduling the next one. Booking again inside three months is usually avoidance wearing the clothes of commitment.
  • Deciding it fixed everything. The most common way people lose what they gained is concluding the work is finished.
  • Deciding it did nothing. Equally common and equally premature. Give it the ninety days.

When something is actually wrong

Most difficulty afterward resolves. Some does not, and it is important to know the difference. Seek clinical support if you have persistent derealisation or depersonalisation, sleep that has not returned after a couple of weeks, intrusive fear or paranoia, beliefs that are not shifting and that concern the people around you, an inability to function at work or at home, or any thoughts of harming yourself.

None of this means something went irreversibly wrong, and none of it is a failure of character. It means the situation now needs a clinician rather than a practitioner. Say plainly what happened and when. A good doctor will work with the facts.

If you are supporting someone else through this, see scope of competence and referral.
