Consent, power, and money

There is no licensing body in this field. In the absence of one, the ethics have to be written down and published, and then someone has to be willing to be held to them.

What informed consent can honestly mean

Consent is difficult here because the experience cannot be fully described in advance. A person cannot meaningfully consent to something they have no reference point for, and dressing that gap up in confident language makes it worse rather than better.

The honest approach is to be exact about everything that can be described, and candid about what cannot. In practice that means the participant knows, in writing and weeks in advance:

  • What is being offered, where it came from, and what is known about it.
  • The physical and psychological risks in plain language, including the serious and rare ones.
  • Who will be present and what each person is responsible for.
  • Whether touch is part of the practice, what kind, and how to decline or withdraw it at any moment.
  • What happens if they ask to stop.
  • What the refund and withdrawal policy is.
  • That the experience itself cannot be fully predicted or described in advance.

Consent obtained on arrival, after travel and payment, in front of a group that has already committed, is a formality. Consent obtained three weeks earlier, with questions invited, is real.

The power asymmetry

The asymmetry in these settings is larger than almost any other outside a hospital. One person is in an ordinary state and holds the keys, the schedule, the knowledge, and the authority. The other is vulnerable, suggestible, and frequently in no condition to advocate for themselves.

That asymmetry does not end when the experience does. For days and sometimes weeks afterward, the participant may hold the facilitator in a light that has more to do with the experience than with the person. Anyone who treats that regard as earned is already in trouble.

Practical consequences: no romantic or sexual contact between facilitator and participant, and not shortly afterward either. No financial entanglement. No recruiting participants into the organisation while they are still in the glow. No use of what was disclosed during preparation for any purpose other than their care.

Touch

Touch is used in many traditions and is genuinely supportive for some people. It is also the most common site of harm in this field, and it requires the clearest agreements.

What good practice looks like: touch is discussed and agreed in writing before the day, not proposed in the moment. Its purpose and form are specific. The participant can decline entirely with no change in how they are treated. They can withdraw permission at any point, including non-verbally, and the signal for doing so is agreed beforehand. Nobody touches anyone in a way that was not agreed in advance, whatever seems called for at the time.

Money

Money is not the problem. Charging for skilled, demanding work is legitimate. The problem is what money does to judgement when nobody has separated the two.

The structural questions worth asking of any organisation, including your own: does the person who screens participants benefit financially from their attendance. Is there a full refund available if screening reaches a no. Are there pressure tactics in the sales process, such as countdowns, last-place claims, or discounts that expire. Is there a lifetime price in circulation for repeat attendance. Are participants encouraged toward more ceremonies as the answer to difficulty.

A field that depends on people returning has a built-in incentive not to help them finish. That has to be resisted deliberately.

Dual relationships

Small communities produce tangled roles quickly. The facilitator is also the landlord, the employer, the teacher, the business partner, the friend. Each additional role makes it harder for the participant to say no to any of them.

Total avoidance is unrealistic in a small field. Naming it is not. Where a dual relationship exists, say so, have someone else handle screening and integration, and be alert to how it will be read by everyone else in the room.

Accountability without a regulator

There is no licensing body to complain to, no register to be struck from, and no required insurance. That absence is the field's largest ethical problem and it will not be solved by good intentions.

What can be done in the meantime is unglamorous and specific: publish your standards, name who supervises you, write down your complaints process and who hears a complaint that concerns the founder, carry insurance, keep records, and answer questions about past complaints without defensiveness.

Any organisation unwilling to say who holds it accountable has answered the question.
